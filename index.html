Why the number sticks

You start from it, then stop too soon. When you're unsure, your mind grabs the nearest number as a starting point and adjusts from there. People tend to stop adjusting once the answer feels plausible, which leaves them short, still near where they started.

The question primes you. Asking "more or less than this?" makes you test the idea that the answer might be close to that number. Your mind pulls up facts that fit it, and those facts shape your guess.

Knowing doesn't fix it. You were told up front that the game shows you a number first. Most people are still pulled. Warning people about anchoring barely reduces it.

How to read your score. The gap compares your guesses after high numbers with your guesses after low numbers, relative to the true answers. Zero means the number had no effect. The pull says how far you moved toward the number: 0% means not at all, 100% means you just repeated it.

What this can't tell you. Twelve questions is a small sample, and how much you already knew matters. If you knew an answer cold, the number had nothing to pull on. The history table is more reliable than one run.

The science

  • The original wheel of fortune. People watched a wheel stop on 10 or 65, then estimated the share of African countries in the United Nations. The low group's typical guess was 25%, the high group's 45%. The wheel was rigged, and it still moved them. Tversky and Kahneman, 1974
  • Even absurd numbers work. Asked whether Gandhi died before or after age 9, people then guessed he died at about 50 on average. Asked about age 140, they guessed about 67. He died at 78. Strack and Mussweiler, 1997
  • Experts are not immune. Real estate agents valued the same house higher when its listing price was higher. Most said they hadn't used the listing price at all. Northcraft and Neale, 1987
  • Not even judges. Experienced judges rolled loaded dice that landed on 3 or 9, then sentenced a hypothetical shoplifter. Those who rolled 9 gave longer sentences, about 8 months versus 5. Englich, Mussweiler and Strack, 2006
  • It holds up. In a large project that re-ran classic psychology studies across dozens of labs, anchoring was one of the strongest and most reliable effects. Klein and colleagues, Many Labs, 2014
  • Warnings barely help. Telling people about anchoring in advance did little to reduce it. Wilson and colleagues, 1996
  • First offers anchor deals. In negotiations, whoever makes the first offer tends to pull the final price toward it. Thinking about the other side's alternatives weakened the pull. Galinsky and Mussweiler, 2001
  • Not every famous result is as strong as reported. A well-known study found people with higher Social Security digits bid more for products. Later replications found a much smaller effect. Anchoring is real, but its size depends on the setting. Ariely, Loewenstein and Prelec, 2003; Maniadis, Tufano and List, 2014

What's still debated. Researchers disagree on the main cause. One view is that we adjust from the number and stop too early. Another is that the "more or less" question makes facts near the number easier to recall. Studies support both, and they likely work together.

Based on Tversky and Kahneman 1974, Strack and Mussweiler 1997, and the Many Labs replication project.
