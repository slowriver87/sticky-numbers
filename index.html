Sticky Numbers

12 quick estimates. Before each one, the game shows you a number.

About 2 minutes

Say if the answer is more or less than the number shown, then guess.

How to play

For each question, the game first shows a number and asks if the true answer is more or less. Then you type your own best guess. No looking things up. Rough guesses are fine.

Each round uses 12 questions you haven't seen before. The results explain how the numbers were picked.

To stop partway, hold the Quit button for one second. On a computer, press Esc twice.

The science behind this game

What it measures

This game measures anchoring: how much a number you've just seen pulls your estimate toward it, even when the number is random. Your score, the pull, is how far your guesses moved toward the game's numbers. 0% means no effect. 100% means you copied them.

How this compares to the lab. Researchers often use an "anchoring index." It compares groups of people who saw a high anchor with groups who saw a low one, and asks how much of the gap between the anchors shows up in their answers [7][8]. This game uses the same idea for one person, across 12 questions: 6 with a high number and 6 with a low one. It measures distances on a ratio scale, so being off by double counts the same for a small answer as for a big one. That's a small difference from the lab version.

What the research says

Even a spinning wheel works. People watched a wheel of fortune give a number, then estimated the share of African countries in the United Nations. Those who saw 10 gave a median guess of 25%. Those who saw 65 gave a median of 45% [1].

Even absurd numbers work. Asked whether Gandhi was older or younger than 140 when he died, people's average guess was 66.7. Asked about 9, the average was 50.1 [2].

The question primes you. One explanation is that comparing the answer with the anchor brings anchor-consistent facts to mind, which then shape your estimate [2].

Experience doesn't protect you. Junior lawyers rolled dice that were rigged to suggest a short or long sentence. Those who saw the higher number gave longer sentences on average, 7.81 months versus 5.28. Across these studies, expertise and experience didn't reduce the effect [3].

Warnings help only a little. Telling people about anchoring in advance helped somewhat, but didn't undo the effect [4].

First offers anchor deals. In negotiations, the first offer strongly predicted the final price. Thinking about the other side's alternatives, or about your own target, cancelled that effect [5].

Adult norms

One large online experiment found an average anchoring index of 0.61, and it reports an earlier classic study finding 0.49 [8]. So people typically move roughly halfway toward an anchor. The 49% to 61% range used in this game is our summary of those two figures, not an official norm. Lab studies set anchors differently, so treat the comparison as approximate.

What's still debated

Researchers disagree on why anchoring happens. One view is that people start from the anchor and stop adjusting once an answer seems plausible. That has been shown for anchors people come up with themselves [6]. Another is the priming explanation above [2]. They may work together.

Not every famous result is as strong as first reported. A well-known study showed that arbitrary numbers, sometimes taken from people's Social Security numbers, shaped how much they valued things [9]. When researchers re-ran a different experiment from that study, the effect was less than a third the size [10]. Anchoring itself is well established. How big it is depends on the setting.

Limits of this game

One round is noisy. In our simulation of 5,000 rounds by players with a known pull, one round's score landed within about ±31 points of the true value 95% of the time. Most of that luck comes from how much you happen to know about the 12 questions. Pooling 7 rounds narrows it to about ±12 points. The scoring itself showed no lean high or low.

Questions can only be fresh once. The bank has 84 questions, enough for 7 rounds. After that, repeats test memory, so they don't count. Rounds played before this update all used the same 12 questions, so only the first of those counts.

Knowledge matters. If you know an answer cold, the number has nothing to pull on. The simulation's knowledge levels are our assumptions, not measured data.
